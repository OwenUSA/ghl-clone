# Roof Leak Playbook: Diagnose, Explain, Report

**Purpose.** Use this to work out what is wrong with a customer's roof or house, and to write a simple, visual report the homeowner understands.
**Compiled.** 2026-11-06, from deep research: IRC code, NRCA, ARMA, IBHS, FEMA, DOE/PNNL, Building Science Corp, Haag Engineering, InterNACHI, ASHI, EPA, FTC/BBB, manufacturer manuals, and report software (CompanyCam, Spectora, SumoQuote, RoofSnap).
**Source.** Researched and written by a separate Claude session for Dream Team Roofing (2026-11-06). Copied here as Owen AI's diagnosis and report reference; the Dream Team-specific report rules are in `inspection-report-guide.md`, which wins when the two differ.

---

## 0. Core rules (read first)

1. **The stain is almost never directly under the hole.** Water that gets past the shingles runs *downhill* along the underlayment, the underside of the roof deck, rafters, nails, pipes and wires. It drips at a low point (a knot, nail, joint or light fixture), often **several feet downslope** of where it got in. Always trace **upslope** from the highest wet spot.
2. **Most leaks are at joints, not in the open field of shingles.** The joints are pipe boots, wall flashing, chimneys, skylights, valleys and vents. No reliable statistic exists; the often-quoted "90 % is flashing, per NRCA" has no traceable source. Field experience still puts **flashing and penetrations first**.
3. **Not every ceiling stain is a roof leak.** Rule out attic condensation, bath-fan ducts, HVAC condensate, plumbing and windows before blaming the roof.
4. **When it leaks tells you where it leaks.** The interview (section 1) is the most valuable diagnostic step.
5. **Separate facts from opinion** in the report: *what we saw* (what a camera would record), *likely cause* (professional opinion), *recommendation*.

## 1. Diagnosis workflow

### Step 1: Interview the customer
Ask: Where is the stain? When did it first appear? Does it grow? What was the weather when it showed up? Is there a bathroom, laundry, AC unit or skylight above it? Has any recent work been done (roof, solar, satellite dish, siding, HVAC)? How old is the roof?

| When it leaks | Most likely causes |
|---|---|
| Every rain | Open hole, cracked pipe boot, failed flashing, missing shingle |
| Only heavy or long rain | Valley, marginal flashing, gutter overflow, missing drip edge |
| Only wind-driven rain from one direction | Ridge or box vents, sidewall or window flashing on the windward side, siding |
| Only during snow melt or thaw | Ice dam (stains at the top of exterior walls) or melting attic frost |
| Cold sunny mornings, no rain | Attic condensation ("attic rain"), skylight condensation |
| Hot humid days or when the AC runs | HVAC duct sweating or condensate pan overflow (not the roof) |
| After showers | Bath-fan duct condensation (not the roof) |
| Any time, including dry weather | Plumbing (not the roof) |
| After a storm | Wind (missing or creased shingles), hail, tree impact |

### Step 2: Inside the house
Map the stain (room, size). Mark its edge in pencil so you can tell later if it grows. Note what is above it (bathroom, AC, skylight, chimney, exterior wall). Use a moisture meter: wood at about 19–20 % moisture or more is at risk of mold, and about 28 % or more means rot is active.

### Step 3: Attic (ideally during or right after rain)
Flashlight up. Look for wet or stained deck, drip marks on insulation, rusty or frosted nail tips, daylight, mold, and wet insulation. Trace the trail **upslope** from the highest wet spot to the penetration or joint.
- **Widespread frost or wetness, frosty nail tips, worst on the north slope** → condensation, not a hole.
- **One localized trail from a penetration** → leak.

### Step 4: Roof exterior (ground and binoculars first; walk only if trained and safe)
Check the penetrations and joints above the attic trail: pipe boots, step flashing, kickout, chimney, skylight, valley, vents, mounts. Then check the general condition of the shingles.

### Step 5: Confirm with a water test (bottom-up)
One person hoses one zone at a time while another watches the attic or ceiling. **Start at the lowest point and work uphill.** Hold each zone 3–10 minutes, and test penetrations separately from the field. Never flood the whole roof. A leak that reproduces gives a confirmed cause.

Other tools:
- **Infrared camera:** finds wet areas but not the entry point. Confirm with a meter.
- **Electronic leak detection** (ASTM D7877): flat membrane roofs only.
- **Smoke testing:** flat roofs only.
- ASTM D5957 flood testing is **not** for roofs. It covers plaza decks.

### Step 6: Decide repair vs replace
- **Repair:** roof under about 15 years old, damage localized, repair under about 30 % of the cost of replacement.
- **Replace:**
  - asphalt about 20 years or older
  - more than 25–30 % damaged
  - widespread curling or granule loss
  - soft deck
  - leaks recurring in several areas
- **Re-cover** (shingles over the old layer): only with ONE existing layer, a dry, sound deck, and never over tile or slate.

## 2. Symptom → cause matrix

| What you see | Look at |
|---|---|
| Stain under a bathroom, laundry or kitchen | Pipe boot above it, or plumbing |
| Stain at the top of an exterior wall or window head | Ice dam, gutter backing up, missing drip edge, window flashing |
| Stain or rot on the wall below where a roof edge meets a wall | Missing kickout flashing |
| Stain on a wall or ceiling beside a fireplace or chimney | Chimney flashing, counterflashing, cricket, masonry |
| Stain at an inside corner of a room | Valley above it |
| Drips around the skylight well | Skylight flashing (if fogged inside on cold days: condensation) |
| Wet insulation directly under the ridge | Ridge vent with wind-driven rain |
| Stains along duct lines or near the air handler | HVAC sweating or condensate overflow |
| Water dripping from the bath-fan grille | Bath-fan duct condensation |
| Many small scattered drips, rusty nail tips | Nail pops, or condensation if widespread |
| Granules piling up in gutters, bald spots | Worn-out shingles, or hail |
| Shingles in the yard, creased tabs | Wind damage |
| Round soft bruises plus dents in gutters and vents | Hail |
| Water standing more than 48 hours on a flat roof | Ponding: drainage or deck sag; check seams |

## 3. Cause cards

Each card gives: what fails, signs, confirmation, fix, typical cost (2025–26 national ranges), urgency, plain explanation and analogy for the customer. The urgency scale is in section 5.

### 3.1 Cracked pipe boot (plumbing vent seal)
- **Fails:** the rubber (neoprene) collar cracks from UV after about 8–15 years (shorter in hot or sunny climates).
- **Signs:** split collar or a gap at the pipe, caulk smeared on it, a stain near a bathroom or laundry.
- **Confirm:** hose the pipe; look for a wet trail in the attic down the pipe.
- **Fix:** replace the boot. The upper flange goes UNDER the upslope shingles and the lower flange OVER the downslope ones. Caulk is not a fix. Check every boot, because they fail together.
- **Cost:** $150–$350 per boot ($75–$600 range). Doing 3–5 at once adds only about $100–$200.
- **Urgency:** Fix soon. Fix now if actively dripping.
- **Customer:** "The rubber seal around a vent pipe on your roof cracked from years of sun. Rain runs down the pipe, slips through the crack and drips into the attic."
- **Analogy:** a turtleneck around the pipe; the collar split, like an old rubber band.

### 3.2 Step and sidewall flashing (roof meets a wall)
- **Fails:** pieces missing, too small, reused, rusted, overlapped the wrong way, or caulk used instead of flashing. Code: at least 4×4 in, one piece per shingle course, nailed to the roof only, siding about 1 in above the roof.
- **Signs:** stains, rot or peeling paint on the wall below the joint. Vinyl, fiber-cement and brick can hide it for years.
- **Fix:** remove the siding, install new step flashing woven in with each course, add a membrane, lap the house wrap over the flashing, reinstall the siding.
- **Cost:** $300–$1,500+ (more with rot).
- **Urgency:** Fix soon. The damage is hidden and keeps spreading.
- **Customer:** "Where your roof meets the wall, small metal pieces should keep water out. They're missing or failed, so water runs behind your siding into the wall."
- **Analogy:** the metal pieces overlap like shingles; one gap and water slips in.

### 3.3 Missing kickout flashing
- **Fails:** there is no diverter where a roof edge ends against a wall, so all the water from the roof-wall joint dumps behind the siding. Code IRC R903.2.1 requires a diverter. It is very often missing.
- **Signs:** streaks or rot below the gutter end, soft siding or stucco, a stain inside on the wall below that corner.
- **Fix:** one-piece kickout as the first step flashing, fastened to the deck only (not the wall). Remove siding locally and repair rot.
- **Cost:** $250–$1,000+ (mostly labor and rot repair).
- **Urgency:** Fix soon. Often severe before anyone sees it.
- **Analogy:** a diving board that throws water into the gutter instead of down the wall.

### 3.4 Chimney leak
- **Fails:** counterflashing caulked to the face of the brick instead of set into a mortar joint, cracked sealant, cracked crown or cap, porous brick or mortar, no cricket on the uphill side. Code requires a cricket when the chimney is more than 30 in wide.
- **Signs:** stain beside the chimney breast or fireplace, efflorescence, spalling brick, water pooling behind the chimney.
- **Fix:** apron flashing → step flashing up both sides → back pan or cricket → counterflashing cut at least 1 in into the mortar joints, stepped, lapping the step flashing by at least 2 in. Repair the crown and mortar. Water repellent does NOT fix failed flashing.
- **Cost:** $500–$1,500 to re-flash; up to $3,500+ with masonry work.
- **Urgency:** Fix soon.
- **Analogy:** the flashing is the chimney's raincoat collar; the counterflashing is the hat brim that keeps water out of the collar. The cricket splits the water like the bow of a ship.

### 3.5 Skylight
- **Leak vs condensation:**
  - A leak gets worse in heavy rain. It usually comes from flashing that was reused during a reroof, or the wrong flashing kit.
  - **Condensation** is fog or drops on the inside glass on cold days, after showers or cooking. VELUX says "condensation is not a leak."
  - Fog *between* the panes means a failed seal; replace the glass unit.
- **Fix:** install the manufacturer's flashing kit for that model. Replace old or fogged units during a reroof. For condensation, keep indoor humidity at 30–50 % and insulate and air-seal the skylight shaft.
- **Cost:** $225–$800 for flashing. Up to about $1,400 depending on cause.

### 3.6 Valley
- **Fails:** nails too close to the centerline, no ice-and-water membrane, uncut corners, a woven valley on a low pitch, debris dams. The valley carries the most water on the roof, and it is flatter than the roof planes on either side.
- **Signs:** granule washout, debris, cracked shingles in the valley, a stain at an interior corner.
- **Fix:** rebuild the valley with a membrane plus a metal liner (open valley, at least 24 in wide) or a proper closed-cut valley (cut 2 in from center, nails at least 6 in from center). That means opening 18–36 in on each side.
- **Cost:** $400–$1,500.
- **Analogy:** a river channel; small flaws become big leaks.

### 3.7 Missing or wind-damaged shingles
- **Fails:** the sealant strip was never bonded or has aged, so wind lifts, creases or removes shingles. Once water gets in, IBHS found total losses run **4–7×** the cost of the roof cover.
- **Signs:** shingles in the yard, exposed underlayment, a horizontal **crease line** across a tab (that counts as damaged even if the shingle is still there).
- **Fix:** replace, nail in the manufacturer's nailing zone (4 nails, 6 in high wind), hand-seal with asphalt cement.
- **Cost:** $150–$1,000 for small areas.
- **Urgency:** **Fix now.** Tarp right away.
- **Analogy:** shingles overlap like fish scales; missing scales let water straight through.

### 3.8 Nail pops and exposed nails
- **Fails:** the deck swells and shrinks, pushing nails up. Short, smooth or under-driven nails make it worse. The raised nail lifts the shingle and breaks its seal.
- **Signs:** bumps under shingles, rust or wet rings around nail tips in the attic. **A cluster of pops means a damp deck or poor ventilation.**
- **Fix:** pull the nail, re-nail about 1 in away with a ring-shank nail, seal, replace damaged shingles. Fix the cause if pops are widespread.
- **Cost:** $150–$400.
- **Urgency:** Watch, or fix soon if many.
- **Analogy:** like a splinter working its way out.

### 3.9 Ice dam
- **Mechanism:** freezing outside + a warm roof deck + snow. Snow melts and refreezes at the cold eave, and the water pond behind the ice backs up under the shingles. The **root cause is heat and air leaking from the house into the attic.**
- **Signs:** icicles, an ice ridge at the eave, leaks only during thaw, stains at the top of exterior walls, window heads and the ceiling perimeter.
- **Fix now:** professional steam removal ($400–$600/hr). **Never** chip, pressure-wash or use rock salt.
- **Fix for good:** air-seal the attic floor, add insulation, keep the soffit-to-ridge vents clear, and add an ice barrier (eave to 24 in inside the wall line) at the next reroof.
- **Urgency:** Fix now while it is actively leaking.
- **Analogy:** a beaver dam at the gutter. The cure is in the attic, not on the roof.

### 3.10 Wind-driven rain through roof vents
- **Fails:** ridge slot cut too wide, unbaffled vents, unflashed box vents, or **mixed exhaust types** (ridge + gable + box or power fan) that short-circuit and pull weather in.
- **Signs:** leaks only with wind from one direction, wet insulation directly under the ridge, a line of snow under the ridge.
- **Fix:** baffled, weather-rated vent; correct the slot (about 1 in each side, stopped 6 in from the ends); remove conflicting vents. Code ventilation is 1/150 of attic area (or 1/300 if split high and low), with intake at least equal to exhaust.
- **Cost:** $250–$900.

### 3.11 Attic condensation ("attic rain"), NOT a roof hole
- **Mechanism:** warm, damp indoor air leaks into the cold attic, freezes as frost on the deck and nails, then melts and drips on sunny thaw days.
- **Signs:**
  - no rain needed
  - widespread wetness rather than one trail
  - frost or rust on nail tips
  - black mold on the sheathing, often on the north slope
  - bath fans venting into the attic
  - blocked soffits
- **Fix:** air-seal the ceiling plane (lights, hatch, pipes), duct bath fans outdoors, clear the vents, keep indoor humidity at 30–50 %. Attic power fans make it worse.
- **Cost:** $300–$2,000.
- **Analogy:** a cold glass of iced tea sweating; the water comes from inside the house, not the sky.

### 3.12 Hail
- **Damage (Haag):** a **bruise** (fractured mat, soft like an apple bruise), a puncture, or granule loss exposing the asphalt. Thresholds are about 1 in hail for 3-tab and about 1¼ in for laminated shingles. Chipped edges alone don't count.
- **Telling hail apart from other marks:**

| Feature | Hail | Blister | Foot or tool | Faked damage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feel | Soft spot | Firm | Firm | Varies |
| Shape | Round, shallow, granules pushed in | Steep-sided pit | Scuff with a ridge of displaced granules | Crushed granules, claw or coin marks |
| Pattern | Random, many sizes, storm-facing slopes | All slopes, uniform size | Along walking paths, around HVAC | Clustered within one person's reach, one per tab |
| Also check | Dents on soft metals (gutters, vents, AC fins) | n/a | n/a | n/a |

- **Fix:** replace the slopes or the roof. This is usually an insurance claim; tell the customer to file promptly because policies have deadlines.
- **Analogy:** a bruised apple; it looks fine outside but is broken inside and will fail later.

### 3.13 Gutter overflow / missing drip edge
- **Fails:** clogged or poorly sloped gutters, a gutter hung over the drip edge, or no drip edge. Code requires drip edge at eaves and rakes, extending at least ¼ in below the deck.
- **Signs:** water pouring between the gutter and fascia, stains *behind* the gutter, soft fascia, wet wall tops or basement.
- **Fix:** clean and re-pitch the gutters (¼ in per 10 ft), add drip edge or a gutter apron, replace rotted fascia, discharge water at least 5 ft from the foundation.
- **Cost:** $200–$600 plus wood repair.
- **Analogy:** the drip edge is the lip on a pitcher.

### 3.14 Worn-out roof (end of life)
- **Signs:** widespread curling (cupping or clawing), cracking, bald spots with exposed asphalt, brittle tabs, granules piling in gutters, multiple leaks.
- **Life by material:**

| Material | Typical life |
|---|---|
| 3-tab asphalt | 15–20 yrs |
| Architectural asphalt | 25–30 yrs (less in hot climates) |
| Metal | 40–70 yrs |
| Tile | 50–100+ yrs (underlayment 20–30) |
| Slate | 60–200 yrs |
| Flat membranes | 15–25 yrs |

- **Fix:** patch today's leak, and recommend planning a replacement. More leaks are likely.
- **Analogy:** an old raincoat; patch one hole and new ones keep opening.

### 3.15 Flat / low-slope roof
- **Fails:**
  - **Ponding** (water standing more than 48 hours, by NRCA's rule; it often voids the warranty)
  - open seams or lifted welds
  - blisters
  - alligator cracking
  - EPDM shrinkage pulling at the edges
  - punctures from foot traffic or HVAC service
- **Fix:**
  - **EPDM:** clean, prime, then cover tape at least 3 in past the damage. Never asphalt products.
  - **TPO:** hot-air weld a patch at least 2 in past the damage.
  - Clear drains and fix the slope.
- **Cost:** $200–$1,000 per seam.
- **Note:** water travels sideways under the membrane, so the entry point can be far from the stain.

### 3.16 NOT a roof leak (look-alikes)
- **Plumbing:** stain in dry weather, under a wet room, grows after using a fixture, stops when the valve is shut.
- **HVAC:** stains along ducts or under the air handler, worse on hot humid days. A clogged condensate line overflows the pan. **Turn the AC off and call an HVAC technician.**
- **Bath fan:** drips from the fan grille after showers.
- **Window or siding:** stain at the head of a window or the top of a wall, only with wind-driven rain.
- **Customer:** "Good news: your roof isn't the problem. The water comes from ___. Here's who to call."

### 3.17 Tree or impact damage
- **Fix:** tarp immediately, remove the branch, replace the deck and any broken framing, re-roof the area. **Keep people out from under damaged framing.**
- **Cost:**

| Damage | Cost |
|---|---|
| Shingles only | $300–$1,500 |
| Punctured deck | $1,500–$8,000 |
| Broken rafters | $8,000–$20,000+ |

### 3.18 Satellite dish / solar / antenna mounts
- **Fails:** lag bolts loosened by wind vibration, holes only caulked, holes left open after removal.
- **Fix:** re-secure with flashed mounts, seal old holes under properly flashed shingles.
- **Cost:** $200–$700.

## 4. Secondary damage and what the customer should do now

- **Mold:** dry wet materials within **24–48 hours** (EPA). Fix the water source first. Mold over about 10 sq ft needs a professional.
- **Insulation:** wet insulation loses R-value. Soaked insulation is usually replaced.
- **Drywall:** a bulging ceiling can hold a lot of water and collapse.
- **Electrical:** wet outlets, switches and fixtures should be inspected or replaced (ESFI). Do not reset tripped breakers.

**Homeowner checklist (use in the report):**
1. Stay out from under a sagging ceiling.
2. Catch the water; move valuables.
3. If a ceiling bulges with no fixture in it, put a bucket under it and poke a small hole at the lowest point.
4. Wet light or outlet: turn off that circuit from a dry spot; call an electrician.
5. Dry within 24–48 hours with fans and a dehumidifier.
6. Photograph everything before cleanup; keep receipts.
7. Mark the edge of the stain in pencil and call if it grows.
8. Don't climb on the roof.

**Free tarps:** after declared disasters, FEMA/USACE "Operation Blue Roof" provides them.

## 5. Severity scale (use exactly three levels, always the same colors)

| Level | Color | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fix now** | Red | Water entering, or a safety risk | Active drip, missing shingles, tree hole, ice dam leaking, sagging ceiling, wet electrical |
| **Fix soon** | Orange | Fix within weeks, before the next storm season | Cracked boot not yet dripping, missing kickout, chimney flashing, worn roof |
| **Watch / maintain** | Blue | No leak yet; monitor or maintain | Isolated nail pop, algae streaks, minor granule loss, gutters needing cleaning |

The overall report status = the worst level found. If nothing is wrong: green, "No roof problem found."

## 6. Photo protocol

1. **House, wide.** Front with the address visible, then the affected elevation (label N/S/E/W).
2. **Problem area, medium.** For example the chimney, pipe or valley in context.
3. **Close-up.** Arrow or chalk circle on the defect, **tape measure or coin for scale**.
4. **Attic.** Wide down the space, then tight on wet wood, stains or wet nail tips. Pair an interior "looking up" shot with an attic "looking down" shot to prove the water path.
5. **Inside.** The stained room wide, then a close-up with a ruler. Note moisture meter readings.
6. **After the repair.** The SAME angle as the "before" close-up.

**Rules:**
- Number every photo, and reference the photo number in each finding.
- Keep the originals with their timestamp and GPS. Never screenshot, because that strips the data.
- Annotate a copy, not the original. Never stage or move debris before shooting.
- Frame interior shots to avoid people, documents and personal items.
- Get written consent before using photos or drone images in marketing.

## 7. Customer report template

**Writing rules:**
- **6th–8th grade reading level** (AHRQ: the average adult reads at about 8th grade; aim for 5th–6th for health-style docs).
- Short sentences. Define every technical word once.
- Numbers instead of adjectives ("a 14-inch stain", not "significant damage").
- One idea per block. Photos bigger than text. Every claim tied to a photo number.
- Story order: **what we found → why it happened → what it caused → how we fix it → how to prevent it.**
- 40 % of homeowners say poor communication is their biggest problem with roofers (Roofing Contractor 2025 survey), more than workmanship (25 %). Clarity is a competitive edge.

**Structure (1–3 pages):**

**Page 1: one-glance page** (most customers only read this)
1. Header: company, phone, license number; customer, address; date; technician; weather; how the roof was inspected (on the roof / ladder / ground / drone / attic / hose test).
2. **Status badge:** red, orange or blue, with one sentence.
3. **What happened**, in 2–3 plain sentences.
4. Hero photo pair: annotated close-up of the cause + the interior stain.
5. What we did today (for example a temporary seal or tarp) + ONE call to action.

**Page 2: the visual story**
6. "Why the stain isn't under the hole" diagram: (1) water gets in → (2) runs along the wood → (3) drips onto the ceiling.
7. One finding card per cause:
   - drawing
   - severity pill
   - **What we saw** (facts)
   - **What it means** (plain)
   - **Why it happens**
   - **Think of it like this** (analogy)
   - **How we fix it**
   - **How to prevent it**
   - photo numbers
8. Damage inside the home checklist (stain, wet drywall, wet insulation, soft deck, rot, wall, mold risk, electrical).

**Page 3: options and protection**
9. Options table, good / better / best. For example: fix the leak · fix it + prevent the next one (recommended) · replace. Show what's included, expected life and price.
10. Before & after photos from the same angle.
11. What to do until it's fixed (section 4 checklist).
12. How your roof keeps water out (6 analogies, below) + words to know.
13. Warranty and insurance (safe wording, below).
14. About this report: limits, plus areas not inspected and why.
15. Signatures + "Questions? Call ___."

**Six "how your roof works" analogies:**
- **Shingles:** fish scales.
- **Underlayment:** a raincoat under your jacket.
- **Roof deck:** the walls of a tent.
- **Flashing:** a raincoat's collar and cuffs.
- **Ventilation:** opening a window after a shower.
- **Gutters:** a pitcher's spout.

## 8. Legal and ethical wording

- **Never promise insurance coverage**, and never offer to waive or absorb a deductible. Deductible waivers are insurance fraud and illegal in TX, FL, OK and other states.
- **Don't negotiate the claim or interpret the policy** unless you are a licensed public adjuster. It is unauthorized public adjusting (TX Ins. Code 4102.163, FL 626.854).
- **Safe insurance wording:** "Whether insurance pays depends on your policy and your adjuster's decision. Sudden storm damage is often covered; wear and age usually are not. We can give your adjuster these photos. We cannot promise coverage or waive your deductible."
- **Limits wording:** "This was a visual inspection of the areas we could safely reach on [date]. We could not see inside walls or under shingles we did not remove. Some leaks only happen in certain weather. This report describes what we found that day; it is not a promise that the roof will never leak."
- Always list **areas not inspected and why.** Silence is liability.
- Use "functional" vs "cosmetic" damage accurately. Functional damage impairs water-shedding or service life; cosmetic is looks only.
- Have a local attorney review the final disclaimer language.
